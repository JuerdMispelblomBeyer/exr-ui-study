# Composition, grid, alignment, density, and whitespace

Analyze the spatial decisions that organize attention. Do not reduce
composition to a list of visible sections.

## Composition map

At thumbnail scale, identify:

1. outer silhouette or chassis;
2. dominant mass;
3. supporting mass;
4. primary axis or directional flow;
5. interruptions, overlaps, crops, and continuations;
6. dense and sparse zones.

Then ask what product or narrative purpose each spatial decision serves. A
large panel is not meaningful until its dominance is linked to a task, object,
or message.

## Grid inference

Trace repeated left/right edges, centers, baselines, and gutters. Record:

- strict versus fluid behavior;
- apparent columns or modular spans, with confidence;
- full-bleed elements and intentional grid breaks;
- nesting and subgrids;
- whether content aligns optically or mathematically;
- how the grid might change at a different viewport, only if visible evidence
  suggests a rule.

Do not reverse-engineer exact pixel values from a scaled screenshot. The useful
principle is often `shared left axis with one controlled break`, not `24 px
gutter`.

## Alignment analysis

Identify alignment as a hierarchy tool:

- shared edges create continuity;
- inset edges create ownership;
- centered axes create ceremony or comparison;
- deliberate misalignment creates tension or editorial rhythm;
- attached edges imply interaction or dependency.

Distinguish deliberate irregularity from accidental near-alignment. Transfer
the rule, not the source's coordinates.

## Density map

Label regions `sparse`, `moderate`, or `dense`. Record:

- whether density is uniform or alternates rhythmically;
- where detail is concentrated;
- whether dense regions are evidence, controls, narrative, or decoration;
- how quickly the reference can be scanned;
- whether empty areas preserve focus or waste space.

The transferable principle should connect density to a mode of use. For
example, `keep orientation sparse and concentrate operational detail inside the
selected object` is transferable; `use lots of whitespace` is not.

## Whitespace ownership

Assign each large empty region a function:

- focus around a dominant object;
- separation between semantic groups;
- editorial pacing;
- affordance for motion or expansion;
- compositional balance;
- or accidental unused area.

Whitespace worth borrowing has a clear owner. Do not transfer ceremonial
emptiness from a marketing case study into a dense operational product without
testing the user's information needs.

## Required analysis blocks

Create observation/interpretation/transfer blocks for:

- overall composition and dominant object;
- grid behavior;
- alignment logic;
- density rhythm;
- whitespace ownership.

For each, state what must not be copied: exact proportions, source information
architecture, device frame, editorial crop, or responsive assumption.
